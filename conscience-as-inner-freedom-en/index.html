Conscience as Inner Freedom

Conscience is the capacity to judge what we do and what is asked of us. It is neither an infallible voice nor a mere personal preference: it is where freedom and responsibility meet.

This page is a contemporary philosophical reflection. It does not reconstruct Bogomil doctrine or attribute a modern concept of rights to medieval dissenters. The comparison with history is meant to raise questions, not to construct an invented genealogy.

Conscience is not automatic obedience

An order may come from a law, a religious tradition, a political group, or the community to which we belong. Its age, popularity, or endorsement by an authority does not by itself make it just. Conscience opens a space for examination: What am I being asked to do? Who will bear the consequences? What harm will it cause? Could I defend the same rule if it concerned someone other than me?

This does not mean that every individual impulse overrides shared rules. Conscience can be shaped by fear, self-interest, habit, and prejudice; it therefore needs listening, discussion, and facts. Inner freedom does not mean always declaring oneself right, but refusing to give up responsibility for one’s choices.

When dissent becomes resistance

To dissent is to reject an explanation or a demand judged unjust. To resist also means standing by that judgement in one’s actions, sometimes at a cost. Sources on medieval religious dissent show that departure from official orthodoxy could bring sanctions and repression. They do not always allow us to know the inner motives of the accused, nor to turn them into modern heroes of freedom.

Dissent can take different forms: refusing a rite, criticising a hierarchy, seeking another interpretation, protecting a persecuted person, or challenging a government’s decisions. These actions are not automatically equivalent. To assess them, we must consider circumstances, aims, and consequences for others.

Resisting without reproducing domination

Resistance may arise from a real experience of oppression and, over time, become a new justification for power. Those who have been harmed may seek justice; that does not make every action taken in their name just. The memory of victims deserves respect, but it must not become a licence to punish people who did not commit those crimes.

The ethical test I propose is simple but demanding: does resistance defend human dignity even when it belongs to someone outside the group, an opponent, or a minority? Does it leave room for internal criticism? Does it recognise individual and institutional responsibility without assigning guilt to entire peoples? If it loses these capacities, it risks replacing one form of domination with another.

Identity and collective freedom

Language, memory, religion, and culture can give continuity to a community and protect it from assimilation. For many people they are sources of belonging and dignity. Yet identity is not fixed: it changes over time, contains differences, and is interpreted by people who may disagree.

A people is not free merely because it can resist external pressure. Collective freedom is also measured by how it treats those who dissent within it, those who belong to a minority, and those who tell an uncomfortable version of the past. State sovereignty and individual freedom of conscience are connected dimensions, but they are not interchangeable.

The comparison with the Bogomils and Serbia

Bogomilism enters this project as a historical case of religious dissent attested mainly through hostile sources. Serbia enters as a different political and cultural history, including forms of autonomy, conflict, memory, and internal plurality. We do not claim that the Bogomils transmitted their identity to contemporary Serbia or that there is a direct continuity between them.

The parallel is a philosophical question: what happens to conscience when an authority claims the exclusive right to define truth or belonging? What does it mean to defend one’s dignity without denying the dignity of others? These questions cross historical periods, even though the historical answers are not the same.

What I mean here by “soul”

Here, “soul” is a philosophical and spiritual word for the inner depth from which judgement, memory, and responsibility arise. It is not a scientific measurement, does not describe a quality exclusive to a people, and does not assign superiority to a faith. Freedom of the soul is not the right to dominate; it is the possibility of not surrendering one’s conscience entirely to fear, hatred, or blind obedience.
