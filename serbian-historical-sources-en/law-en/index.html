Documents of the Serbian Academy

Royal charters and digital research

The University of Graz MeSeCA project develops a digital edition of medieval Serbian charters, building on a database initiated at the Serbian Academy. The corpus comprises around 600 documentary units and supports research into sovereignty, monasteries and diplomacy.

Medieval law and limits on power

Articles 171 and 172 of Dušan’s Code concern the relationship between law, imperial will and judicial decisions. For Byzantine context and the limits of modern constitutional readings, see Srđan Šarkić (2023), pp. 209–221.

Annotated translations — Dušan’s Code, Article 172

Article 172 — On judges

Let all judges judge according to the code, rightly, as written in the code, and let them not judge out of fear of my imperial majesty.

Working English translation from the Serbian text reproduced in the online source. Judges are directed to follow the law rather than fear the ruler. The preceding article 171 addresses imperial orders contrary to the law. This provision belongs to a medieval monarchy, rather than a modern system of separation of powers.

Serbian reference text (not a diplomatic transcription): Викизворник.

Digitised historical edition, pending collation: Stojan Novaković, 1898.

Srđan Šarkić, Constitutional Norms in Serbian Medieval Law (2023), p. 213.

Saint Sava’s Nomocanon: law and religion

Compiled in the early thirteenth century, the Zakonopravilo brings together Byzantine civil and ecclesiastical law. The original is lost. Its earliest surviving witness, copied in 1262 for Bishop Neophite of Zeta, is the Ilovica manuscript, held in Zagreb (HAZU III c 9).

For the history of religious dissent, the Ilovica witness is important for its references to the Babuns, associated with Bogomilism in historical scholarship. Such classifications represent ecclesiastical authorities rather than the direct testimony of the groups described.

Research note, not a translation of the medieval text.

The Dečani chrysobulls (1330–1346)

The first charter, issued by Stefan Uroš III in 1330, establishes the legal status and estates of the monastic foundation. It is held by the Archives of Serbia in Belgrade, R–2. Three fourteenth-century versions are known; the first survives intact on a parchment scroll 5.2 metres long.

The charters illuminate land tenure, inhabitants’ obligations, place names and personal names. Their data reflect estate administration rather than a modern census.

For example, the Austrian Academy of Sciences database records the donation of Grad in Plav to the monastery in 1330 (DH 73).

Milica Grković’s bilingual edition (2004), accessible through the monastery website, includes a medieval transcription and Serbian and English translations. This entry provides an original research guide, not a republication of those translations.

Stefan Lazarević’s Mining Code

The Novo Brdo compilation bears the date 29 January 1412, although the chronology of its constituent legal texts remains debated. An illustrated sixteenth-century copy is held in the Archives of the Serbian Academy of Sciences and Arts (SASA 465).

The code regulates concessions, disputes among miners and urban affairs. Twenty-four specialists, known as ‘good men’, participated in its drafting. Its provisions illustrate the local adaptation of Saxon mining customs and interaction with other European legal traditions.

Research pathway: compare mining concessions with article 123 of Dušan’s Code and distinguish mining regulations from municipal law. This entry is a bibliographical synthesis, not a manuscript translation.

Dušan’s Code, Article 10 — repression of heresy

Working translation from modern Serbian: Whoever is found to be a heretic living among Christians shall be branded on the face and expelled; whoever conceals that person shall also be branded.

The provision of 1349 penalizes both dissenters and those sheltering them. The term heretic does not explicitly identify Bogomils. The law establishes a legal prescription, not the frequency of its enforcement. Comparison with Articles 105, 171 and 172 shows that constraints on imperial authority could coexist with religious coercion.

Dušanov zakonik, čl. 10 (Wikisource) · Šarkić (2023), pp. 212–214

Article 85 — Babun speech

Working translation from modern Serbian: Whoever utters Babun speech, if a nobleman, shall pay one hundred perpers; if a commoner, shall pay twelve perpers and be beaten with sticks.

The provision differentiates penalties by social status. Babun is retained as a historical term: its relationship to Bogomilism requires contextualization and does not establish the beliefs of every accused person. The perper is a historical monetary unit. This translation has not been collated against a manuscript.
