The Lives of Stefan Nemanja (Saint Simeon)

Two of Nemanja’s sons wrote accounts of their father: Sava, in the context of the Studenica Typikon in the early thirteenth century, and Stefan the First-Crowned, by 1216. The former foregrounds monastic experience, the latter dynastic and political themes.

Abdication and monastic life offer a means of examining the relationship between power and spirituality. Both narratives are literary witnesses to be read in context, rather than neutral chronicles.

The surviving Studenica Typikon witness is held at the National Museum Library in Prague, IX H 8 (Š 10). Further reading: P. J. Šafařík, Památky dřevního písemnictví Jihoslovanův, second edition, 1873. Comparative research note, not a complete translation.

Medieval sources on religious dissent and Bogomilism

Stefan the First-Crowned’s Life of Saint Simeon describes an anti-heretical assembly under Nemanja, including corporal punishment, expulsion and book destruction. The hagiographic narrative legitimises the ruler as a defender of Orthodoxy.

Teodosije’s Life of Saint Sava recounts the council at Žiča, generally dated to 1221, emphasising preaching and return to Orthodoxy. Domentijan provides a useful comparison.

Dušan’s Code prescribes penalties against heretics and the expression termed babun. Polemical categories do not automatically identify every condemned person as a Bogomil. Legislation records prescriptions, not necessarily their enforcement.

These sources represent authorities and their authors rather than direct writings by dissenters. This entry offers comparative analysis, not a complete translation.

Dušan’s Code, Article 10 — repression of heresy

Working translation from modern Serbian: Whoever is found to be a heretic living among Christians shall be branded on the face and expelled; whoever conceals that person shall also be branded.

The provision of 1349 penalizes both dissenters and those sheltering them. The term heretic does not explicitly identify Bogomils. The law establishes a legal prescription, not the frequency of its enforcement. Comparison with Articles 105, 171 and 172 shows that constraints on imperial authority could coexist with religious coercion.

Dušanov zakonik, čl. 10 (Wikisource) · Šarkić (2023), pp. 212–214

Presbyter Cosmas and the Serbian reception of the Beseda

Composed in tenth-century Bulgaria, the discourse against heretics is a major polemical witness to Bogomilism. It also addresses monastic discipline and the duties of believers. The Serbian Dragolj miscellany (Rs 651) contains a reworked version entitled О оучениї вѣры (On the teaching of faith). It is neither a Bogomil-authored text nor necessarily identical to the Bulgarian recension.

Dimitrije Bogdanović, Srpska prerada Kozmine besede u zborniku popa Dragolja, Balcanica 7 (1976), pp. 61–90. Bibliographic reference / Библиографски извор

Yurij K. Begunov, study of the Serbian compilation of the Beseda, Slovo 18–19 (1969), pp. 91–108. According to the author’s abstract, the compilation may date to the 1220s–1230s and have supplied clergy with guidance for identifying Bogomils, supplementing Cosmas with scriptural and Pseudo-Athanasian quotations. This is a scholarly hypothesis, not a certain date. The National Library of Serbia dates manuscript Rs 651 to the third quarter of the thirteenth century, whereas Begunov’s abstract gives a later date for the codex. Bogdanović (1976) remains to be consulted in full.

Article 85 — Babun speech

Working translation from modern Serbian: Whoever utters Babun speech, if a nobleman, shall pay one hundred perpers; if a commoner, shall pay twelve perpers and be beaten with sticks.

The provision differentiates penalties by social status. Babun is retained as a historical term: its relationship to Bogomilism requires contextualization and does not establish the beliefs of every accused person. The perper is a historical monetary unit. This translation has not been collated against a manuscript.
