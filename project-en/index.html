The Project

Duhovno nasleđe Balkana — “The Spiritual Heritage of the Balkans” — is an independent cultural project on the religious and spiritual history of the region, with particular attention to Serbia, Bogomilism, and the relationship between conscience and power.

Why this project

The Balkans are often told about only through borders, wars, and national affiliations. This project adds the history of religious ideas, communities, and people who sought to define their faith in relation to the authorities of their time.

The starting point is medieval Bogomilism, attested above all in Bulgaria and discussed in connection with the wider religious networks of the Balkans. From there, the project considers medieval Serbia, the Bosnian Church, and memories of contemporary Serbia. It does not seek a simple genealogy connecting the Bogomils to modern Serbia; instead, it places different periods in dialogue through questions about authority, identity, and responsibility.

Editorial aims

  • Make historical research accessible, with sources and bibliographies that readers can consult.
  • Explain the context and limits of testimonies, especially when they come from polemicists or hostile authorities.
  • Present scholarly debates without turning a hypothesis into an established fact.
  • Keep historical reconstruction distinct from contemporary philosophical reflection.
  • Contribute to respectful knowledge of Serbia and the plurality of the Balkans, without propaganda or collective blame.

Topics

Faith and dissent. This strand examines how chronicles, polemical writings, and ecclesiastical acts describe groups labelled heretical. It asks who produced each testimony, for what purpose, and which claims can be checked against independent evidence. Labels imposed by opponents are not automatically treated as shared identities; any reconstruction of belief remains proportionate to the surviving documentation.

Religion and power. The focus is medieval Serbia and the relationship between dynastic consolidation, ecclesiastical organization, and religious uniformity. Sources concerning the Nemanjić dynasty and condemnations of dissent are read in their political and documentary settings: evidence of repression does not by itself show how widespread a doctrine was or what every person involved believed. Comparison with Bosnia and Bulgaria helps clarify both contact and difference without merging distinct communities.

Memory and conscience. This section addresses the dissolution of Yugoslavia, the conflicts of the 1990s, the 1999 NATO bombing, and subsequent political change. It brings documents, court decisions, reports, and testimony into conversation while identifying each source’s perspective and limits. The responsibility of governments, leaders, and armed groups is examined without assigning collective blame to populations; civilian experiences and conflicting memories remain part of the historical account.

Resistance and dignity. The comparison between medieval religious dissent and contemporary resistance is a philosophical lens, not a claim of direct historical continuity between the Bogomils and modern Serbia. The question is how conscience and communal memory can oppose coercion while preserving the dignity of others. Suffering deserves recognition, but past suffering does not in turn justify domination, violence, or exclusion.

Belonging, memory, and freedom of conscience

The project approaches with respect the connection many Serbs recognize with their history, language, collective memory, traditions, and cultural continuity. Patriotism, understood as care for a community and its heritage, can coexist with plural interpretations, criticism, and freedom of conscience. This perspective does not assume that Serbian society is uniform, nor does it identify a people with the decisions of its governments or institutions.

The comparison with the Bogomils opens a wider question: how can the dignity of conscience be defended when authority demands uniformity? This is a philosophical comparison, not a genealogy linking a medieval movement to modern Serbian identity. The historical pages distinguish testimony from reconstruction; the reflection identifies itself as interpretation.

Method and responsibility

The pages identify the kinds of sources used and, as far as possible, distinguish documentary evidence, historiographical interpretation, and the author’s reflection. Estimates are attributed to the organizations that produced them; controversies are identified; bibliographical links accompany contributions.

The project does not claim to speak on behalf of Serbia or the Balkans. It is not affiliated with a university, public body, or cultural institution. Any collaboration will be announced only after an explicit agreement.
